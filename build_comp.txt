SET - 1 [Q .1. to Q .5.]
The burgeoning field of epigenetics has revolutionized our
understanding of heredity, extending beyond the simplistic
Mendelian model of gene transmission. Traditionally, genetics
focused on the DNA sequence itself as the sole determinant of
an organism's traits. However, epigenetics reveals that
environmental factors, lifestyle choices, and even historical
traumas can profoundly influence gene expression without
altering the underlying DNA code. These epigenetic
modifications – such as DNA methylation and histone
acetylation – act as molecular switches, turning genes on or off,
or modulating their activity levels. This plasticity means that an
identical twin, despite sharing an identical genome with their
sibling, can develop different susceptibilities to disease or even
exhibit distinct personality traits due to divergent epigenetic
landscapes shaped by their individual experiences. Furthermore,
some epigenetic tags can be inherited across generations,
implying that the choices and environments of our ancestors
might subtly predispose us to certain conditions or capabilities.
This intergenerational inheritance challenges long-held notions
of fixed genetic destiny, suggesting a dynamic interplay between
nature and nurture where nurture can directly influence which
parts of our nature are expressed. The implications are
far-reaching, from developing novel therapeutic strategies for
diseases like cancer to reframing public health interventions that
consider transgenerational environmental impacts.
Understanding epigenetics compels us to view biological
inheritance not as a rigid blueprint, but as a malleable score
being continuously re-orchestrated by life itself.

The digital divide, a chasm separating those with access to
modern information and communication technology (ICT) from
those without, poses a significant challenge to equitable
development. While the internet offers unparalleled
opportunities for education, economic growth, and civic
participation, billions globally remain unconnected or lack the
necessary digital literacy to leverage its benefits. This divide is
not merely about access to hardware like smartphones or
computers; it encompasses disparities in broadband
connectivity, affordability, quality of service, and the skills
needed to effectively navigate the digital landscape. In
developed nations, the divide often manifests as a gap between
urban and rural areas, or between different socio-economic
groups. In developing countries, it is a more fundamental barrier,
with entire regions lacking basic infrastructure. The
consequences are profound: those on the wrong side of the
divide face reduced educational opportunities, limited access to
remote work, exclusion from e-governance services, and a
diminished voice in the global conversation. Bridging this gap
requires multi-faceted interventions, including government
initiatives to expand infrastructure, subsidies to make internet
access affordable, and comprehensive digital literacy
programmes. Furthermore, content must be culturally relevant
and multilingual to ensure inclusivity. Ultimately, ensuring
universal access and digital competence is not just about
technology; it's about fostering social equality and ensuring that
no one is left behind in the rapidly advancing digital age.

SET - 3 [Q .11. to Q .15.]
The symbiotic relationship between flowering plants and their
pollinators is a cornerstone of global biodiversity and ecosystem
stability. Pollinators, primarily insects like bees, butterflies, and
moths, but also birds and bats, play a crucial role in the
reproduction of over 80% of the world's flowering plants,
including many food crops essential for human sustenance. As
these animals visit flowers to feed on nectar or pollen, they
inadvertently transfer pollen grains from one flower to another,
enabling fertilization. This intricate dance has evolved over
millions of years, leading to highly specialized adaptations in
both plants and pollinators. For instance, some flowers have
evolved specific shapes, colors, or scents to attract particular
pollinators, while pollinators have developed specialized
mouthparts or behaviors to access the rewards offered by these
flowers.
However, this vital relationship is under severe threat from
various anthropogenic factors. Habitat loss due to urbanization
and agriculture, pesticide use (especially neonicotinoids),
climate change altering bloom times, and the spread of diseases
and invasive species are collectively contributing to a dramatic
decline in pollinator populations worldwide. The consequences
of this decline are far-reaching. Beyond the ecological impact on
wild plant reproduction, there are significant economic
implications for agriculture. Many fruits, vegetables, and nuts
rely heavily on animal pollination, and a reduction in pollinator
services can lead to lower yields, reduced quality, and increased
food costs. Scientists are actively researching solutions, from
promoting pollinator-friendly farming practices and creating
urban pollinator gardens to developing less harmful pest
management strategies and conserving natural habitats.
Understanding and protecting these unsung heroes of our
ecosystems is paramount for food security and the health of our
planet.

The perennial discussion surrounding the value of a liberal arts
education in a technologically driven world often pits practical,
vocational training against broader intellectual development.
Critics argue that in an era demanding specialized skills and
immediate job readiness, degrees in fields like philosophy,
literature, or history are impractical and fail to equip graduates
for the modern workforce. They emphasize the need for STEM
(Science, Technology, Engineering, and Mathematics) fields,
which directly contribute to economic growth and offer clearer
career paths with higher earning potential, asserting that a focus
on specific, marketable skills is the most efficient route to
professional success.
However, proponents of a liberal arts education contend that its
value lies precisely in its capacity to cultivate adaptable, critical
thinkers—qualities that are increasingly vital in a rapidly
changing global landscape. Instead of narrowly focusing on
'what to think,' a liberal arts curriculum teaches 'how to think.' It
exposes students to a wide range of disciplines, fostering
intellectual curiosity, analytical reasoning, effective
communication, and problem-solving abilities that transcend
specific industries or technologies. Graduates learn to interpret
complex information, articulate persuasive arguments,
understand diverse cultural contexts, and approach challenges
from multiple perspectives—skills that are foundational for
leadership, innovation, and civic engagement.
In a world where automation and AI are poised to transform
many specialized jobs, the uniquely human capacities developed
through a liberal arts education—creativity, ethical reasoning,
empathy, and holistic understanding —become more, not less,
valuable. Employers increasingly seek individuals who can adapt
to new challenges, collaborate effectively, and contribute to
organizational culture, rather than merely possessing a narrow
technical skill set that might quickly become obsolete.
Therefore, a liberal arts education, far from being impractical,
provides a robust intellectual toolkit that prepares individuals not
just for a first job, but for a lifetime of learning, career
transitions, and meaningful contributions to society.

With the rapid advancement of digital technology, reading habits
have undergone a dramatic transformation. A few decades ago,
printed books and newspapers were the primary sources of
information and leisure reading. Today, digital screens dominate
our lives, offering instant access to e-books, online articles, and
news portals. While this shift has made reading more
convenient, it has also raised concerns about declining attention
spans and reduced comprehension. Researchers suggest that
reading on screens often leads to superficial engagement, as
readers tend to skim rather than absorb information deeply.
Printed books, on the other hand, encourage focus and retention
through a more immersive experience.
Despite these concerns, digital reading has several undeniable
advantages. It allows users to carry thousands of books in one
device, provides built-in dictionaries for quick reference, and
ensures accessibility for people with visual impairments through
adjustable fonts and audio support. Moreover, digital platforms
promote environmental sustainability by reducing paper
consumption. However, some educators argue that an
overreliance on screens can cause eye strain, fatigue, and even
disrupt sleep patterns due to prolonged exposure to blue light.
Ultimately, the debate between digital and print reading is not
about choosing one over the other, but about achieving balance.
Readers can enjoy the convenience of technology while still
preserving the depth of traditional reading. By practising mindful
reading—whether on paper or screen—individuals can ensure
that technology enhances rather than weakens their intellectual
engagement.

paradoxical position within modern society. It promises
objectivity, progress, and mastery over nature, yet it
simultaneously engenders profound ethical dilemmas. The

widening gap between capability and conscience underscores
the need for an ethical framework that evolves in tandem with
discovery. Historically, science has been regarded as a neutral

inseparable from the act of inquiry.

integrated within its very methodology. The development of
nuclear weapons, surveillance technologies, and environmental
exploitation has demonstrated that knowledge divorced from
moral accountability leads to existential peril. In this sense,
ethical deliberation is not an impediment to progress but its
precondition. The challenge lies in reconciling the empirical rigor
of science with the normative demands of ethics —an endeavor
that requires humility, dialogue, and an awareness of science’s
sociopolitical embeddedness. To imagine science as detached
from values is to ignore the historical record of how power and
ideology have influenced research agendas, from colonial
anthropology to corporate-funded pharmacology. In the twenty -
first century, as humanity stands at the threshold of genetic
redesign and machine consciousness, the integration of ethical
reasoning into scientific practice is no longer optional but
essential. The true measure of scientific progress will thus
depend not merely on discovery, but on discernment—on our
collective ability to wield knowledge without surrendering

Justice and equality, though often invoked together, are distinct
yet interdependent ideals that form the backbone of any
democratic society. Justice concerns itself with fairness —
ensuring that individuals receive what they are due, based on
merit, need, or equity. Equality, on the other hand, demands that
all individuals be treated the same, without discrimination or
privilege. However, absolute equality can sometimes undermine
justice, just as rigid notions of justice may perpetuate inequality.
For instance, providing identical resources to students from
vastly different socio-economic backgrounds may appear equal
but fails to be just, as it ignores contextual disadvantages.
Hence, modern social theorists argue for “equity” — a dynamic
balance that considers differences without abandoning fairness.
Philosophers from Aristotle to Rawls have grappled with this
tension. Aristotle viewed justice as giving each person their due,
emphasizing proportional fairness rather than sameness.
Centuries later, John Rawls introduced the “veil of ignorance” —
a thought experiment suggesting that just principles are those
chosen without knowing one’s social status. His theory
underscored that true fairness must protect the least
advantaged, not merely maintain formal equality. However,
translating such philosophical ideals into public policy is rarely
straightforward. Bureaucratic systems often reduce nuanced
principles into rigid procedures, where equality is measured by
uniform rules rather than outcomes. The digital age adds
another layer to this debate. Algorithms used in hiring, lending,
or law enforcement claim objectivity but often reproduce
existing biases encoded in data. A recruitment system that
screens candidates purely on “neutral” data may still
discriminate against marginalized groups, revealing how equality
in process does not always yield justice in result. Therefore,
achieving a just society demands constant reflection — to move
beyond mechanical equality and toward fairness rooted in
empathy, adaptability, and moral imagination.

The concept of environmental stewardship encompasses more
than mere conservation; it represents a proactive commitment
to sustaining the biosphere through ethical engagement,
scientific insight, and social responsibility. Ecosystems, as
intricate networks of interdependent species and abiotic factors,
demonstrate that ecological stability is contingent upon balance
rather than domination. Historically, human societies that
harmonized their activities with natural rhythms—through
agrarian practices, sacred groves, or community-managed water
systems—exemplified resilience and sustainability. Conversely,
extractive exploitation, often justified by short-term gains, has
repeatedly led to biodiversity loss, soil degradation, and climate
vulnerability. Philosophically, environmental stewardship
interrogates anthropocentric paradigms, challenging the
assumption that human needs automatically supersede
ecological thresholds. By cultivating ecological empathy, it
promotes a recognition of intrinsic value in nonhuman life and a
duty of care that transcends utilitarian calculus. On a
sociopolitical level, stewardship manifests through legislation,
policy frameworks, and civic initiatives that prioritize
environmental justice. Yet, token gestures such as symbolic
tree-planting campaigns or eco-certifications without systemic
reform risk being performative, masking persistent
environmental inequities. Economically, environmental
stewardship drives innovation in renewable energy, sustainable
agriculture, and circular economies. Diverse stakeholder
engagement—from local communities to multinational
corporations—enhances adaptive capacity and problem-solving,
demonstrating that ecological responsibility and economic
vitality are not mutually exclusive. In an era marked by climate
crisis and rapid urbanization, the imperative to sustain
ecological diversity is both urgent and complex. Stewardship,
therefore, is not merely a technical practice; it is an ethical and
existential orientation, underscoring humanity’s intertwined fate
with the natural world.

The human lymphatic system plays a crucial role in maintaining
internal balance by transporting excess fluid, filtering harmful
substances, and supporting immune responses. Unlike the
circulatory system, the lymphatic network does not rely on a
central pump; instead, it depends on muscle movement and
pressure changes to propel lymph throughout the body. Lymph
nodes, positioned strategically along lymphatic vessels, act as
biological checkpoints where pathogens are identified and
neutralised.
Disruptions in this system can lead to a condition known as
lymphoedema, characterised by abnormal fluid accumulation
and persistent swelling, usually in the limbs. Left unmanaged,
lymphoedema may impair mobility and increase susceptibility to
infections. Early diagnosis is therefore essential, as timely
intervention can significantly slow the progression of symptoms.
Health experts emphasise that regular physical activity,
adequate hydration, and proper limb care all contribute to a
healthier lymphatic function. Additionally, avoiding tight clothing,
which may restrict lymph flow, helps maintain natural circulation.
As awareness grows, individuals are encouraged to monitor
changes in their body and seek guidance whenever swelling or
discomfort persists.
